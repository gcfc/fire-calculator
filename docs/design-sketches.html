fire-calculator · design notes

Four sketches: packaging, mortality, flow, and routes

Answers to the four design questions, drawn in the app's own palette and wired to real output from simulate(DEFAULTS) — FIRE age 44.2, bridge $2.60M, horizon 101.

01

Simple planner + separate advanced one, or something else?

One form in three tiers — not two apps

A separate simple mode would have to either ignore housing, kids and the 59½ gate, or silently assume them. The first rebuilds the naive calculator your README exists to argue against; the second hides the assumptions that make your answer differ. Both undercut the product.

The fix is smaller than a second app: collapse the sections, but print their current value in the header. That's the difference between progressive disclosure done well and done badly. Hidden-with-a-silent-default is a trap; collapsed-with-a-visible-summary is a summary the user can audit at a glance and open when they care.

The other three moves worth making

  1. Change the default household. 27 years old with $600k saved and a $2M house is a very specific person. A first-time visitor with $40k saved reads that and leaves. Ship the presets, and make the landing default a median-ish one.
  2. Never let the chart fall below the fold. On mobile you currently stack ~40 inputs above the output. Put the chart first on small screens, or pin a two-line result strip to the top. The live chart is the feedback loop — a user who never sees it react has no reason to keep typing.
  3. Add sliders next to the number fields on tier 1, not instead of them. Numbers are for entering what you know; sliders are for exploring what you don't. Your Advanced panel already has the pattern.

Don't build a wizard. Step-through onboarding hides the chart during input, which kills the one thing that makes this tool worth using. Tiering is vertical, not temporal.

02

How does mortality probability look, and how does it affect the outcome distribution?

Your horizon of 101 is roughly a 7% scenario

Mortality enters as a survival curve S(a) — the probability of still being alive at age a — from the SSA Period Life Table. For a couple you want the last-survivor curve, 1 − (1−S_you)(1−S_partner), because that is exactly the horizon your model already computes as END = endAge + max(0, partnerOffset).

What it does to the numbers

There are three levels of ambition here, and the cheapest one is worth doing on its own.

Level What it is New machinery Effect
1 Survival ribbon on the age axis + a relabelled endAge One lookup table. No model change. Reframes the horizon honestly. Nothing moves.
2 Mortality-weighted Need — discount each year's spend by S(a) One factor inside the existing backward induction. Cuts Need ~8–12%. Leaves Bridge nearly untouched.
3 The full rich / broke / dead stack Requires return distributions — i.e. the backtesting work first. Replaces one date with a distribution.

Level 2 has a result you'll like. Mortality discounting bites hardest on far-future spending, which is what Need is made of. It barely touches Bridge, because the years between retiring at 44 and unlocking at 59½ are ones you're ~97% likely to be alive for. So weighting by survival lowers the wealth constraint and leaves the liquidity constraint standing — the bridge binds harder, not softer.

On your defaults it already binds exactly: fireTaxable and fireBridge are both $2,595,315, to the dollar. Mortality-weighting widens that gap rather than closing it, which strengthens the thesis the whole app is built on.

The caveat to ship alongside it. A mortality-weighted number is an expected cost, and planning to an expectation means a coin-flip's chance of outliving the money. That is why every serious planner sizes to a high percentile instead. Show it as a second readout — "cost if you live to 101: $4.58M · expected cost: $4.1M" — never as a replacement for the number.

While you're there: the field labelled Life Expectancy is misnamed. Life expectancy for a 27-year-old is about 80. The field is a planning horizon, and calling it what it is would stop people entering 80 and quietly underfunding by twenty years.

How would the Sankey diagram look for this?

One year at a time — and the locked channel is the story

A Sankey shows one year's flows: sources on the left, sinks on the right, ribbon width proportional to dollars. Every number below is read straight out of sim.trace on your defaults — no new math, just a second rendering of the table you already ship.

Age 35 — working, both earning

Age 50 — retired, six years in, still locked out

Build notes

  • Add a year scrubber. Dragging across ages makes daycare appear and vanish, the mortgage clear at 61, and the locked channel snap open at 59½. That's the whole plan as one gesture.
  • Flows must balance — which is a feature. Your model permits negative taxable balances, so in an underwater year the sinks exceed the sources. Give the deficit its own coral source node labelled borrowed. A Sankey makes an implicit loan impossible to miss, where the line chart only shades it.
  • Reuse C directly — housing brass, kids coral, taxable liquid, 401k locked-blue. The Sankey then reads as the same object as the chart legend rather than a second visual language.
  • Recharts ships a <Sankey> component, but it's rigid about node placement and won't do the severed-channel treatment above. Given how much bespoke SVG the chart panel already contains, hand-rolling ~40 lines of path math is likely less work than fighting it.
04

Elaborate on scenario landing pages — how do they look, and how is that the same work as presets?

One table of parameter overrides, rendered two ways

A scenario landing page is a real URL — /scenario/dual-income/ — serving a real HTML file whose <title>, meta description and opening prose are all about that one situation, with the calculator underneath it already filled in for that situation. The visitor lands from search on a page that appears to have been built for them, and it was, at a marginal cost of about thirty lines of copy.

Anatomy of one page

Why it's the same work as presets

Because a preset and a landing page are the same record. A preset is {label, params}. A landing page is {label, params} plus copy and a slug. Build the table once and both fall out of it:

You have already written this build script. scripts/build-legal.mjs walks LEGAL_PAGES, renders each to dist/<slug>/index.html with its own title, meta description and robots tag, and cross-links the set in a footer. A scenario generator is that file with SCENARIOS swapped in and the app bundle included instead of inline prose styling. The hard part — clean URLs from a static host without a router — is solved and shipping.

Two things to get right

  1. Pre-render the prose into the HTML. Google executes JavaScript, but inconsistently and on a delay. Since you're generating files at build time anyway, bake the h1, the 250 words and the FAQ into the served markup and let React hydrate around them. This is also what makes the page useful before the bundle parses.
  2. Give the root index.html a description and Open Graph tags. It currently has a title and nothing else — no description, no og:image, no canonical. Every share link anyone has ever sent unfurls as a bare URL. That's a smaller fix than the landing pages and probably worth more per hour.

Keep the count honest. Six to eight scenarios that genuinely differ — dual income, single income, with kids, with a pension, already own a home, late starter — is a content strategy. Forty permutations of the same three fields is doorway-page spam, and Google has an explicit policy against it. The test: does the pre-filled state actually change the answer, and does the prose say something that isn't true of the other pages?
